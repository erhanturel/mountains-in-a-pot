Where it stands

You get one pot. You cut the ground, hang clouds, and the water finds its own level. Life takes hold on the shores it makes. Eight Years, about half an hour.

It is playable now — pot.html is a single file that opens from a double click. No server, no install.

What changed

The pot is generated

Every run used to open on the same flat disc of rock. Now the terrain is made from a seed, and the valleys are cut by our own erosion engine rather than painted on.

was: 1 of 5 rocks visible, 1 of 11 biomes, no relief · now: 4 of 5 rocks, 7–10 elevations of relief
Rain now costs something

Water is the only thing that moves ground, and some of it goes over the edge for good. What it uncovers is granite, which will not make soil again inside a run. Life binds the ground, so planting is the only defence.

2 clouds → 0.25% of the pot lost · 14 clouds → 13%, and 106 hexes turned barren
Score is life times what the pot has left

It used to be life times the shapes the water made. On real terrain the water makes those by itself, so the score was measuring the seed instead of the player.

The world reports on itself

A feed beside the board: “Granite is showing on 4 more hexes. It makes no soil.” Erosion is the one thing that happens to you rather than because of you, and nothing used to say it was happening.

Pacing was one speed for everything

Water, soil and life all moved at nearly the same rate, so every speed button made the whole world fast or the whole world slow. They are spread apart now — water in seconds, life in a minute, soil across the run.

was: 1.6× between fastest and slowest · now: 16.5×
Rock is layered, and erosion can tell the types apart

Five kinds, from soft shale to hard granite. Digging is discovery rather than removing the one material there was.

two halves of one slab under one cloud: 0.002 elevations apart with it off · 6.8 apart with it on
Smaller things

Meteors you can watch land. Waterfalls are drawn at last. Camera on the keyboard. Flickering water fixed. The wheel of fortune waits for you instead of interrupting. “Harvest” is now “The Reading” — nothing is reaped, the year turns and the pot is read out.

The decision the game now has

This is the part worth reading twice. Rain is a loan against the pot, and the interest comes due late in the run.

Before this, everything that made the game hard came from outside the simulation: a spending budget, a target number, a quest list. Now the world itself pushes back.

What's next

  1. Someone plays a full run. The score targets are still set by a script, not a person. This is the next thing that matters and it needs a human.
  2. Climate. Temperature, wind, and clouds that drift instead of sitting where you put them. Rain shadows make deserts on their own. There is finally terrain for it to act on.
  3. Pots you can share. A seed picks the terrain already; it just needs to be offered to the player. This is the answer to “why start a second run”.
  4. Grass that moves in the wind. Last on purpose — polishing while the systems underneath are still moving means doing it twice.

Open for you

Should a cloud rain on seven hexes instead of one?

Stacked clouds drop everything on a single column and pile the water up. Spreading it over the hex below and its six neighbours halves that, and makes shaping the land the way you steer water. It changes a core rule, so it wants a decision rather than a quiet edit.

Is “slag” the right word?

It is the loose material erosion makes. “Regolith” is the true word and the more interesting one; “slag” sounds industrial.

How punishing should the pot's decline be?

Right now the physics decides it and the range is wide — a careless run costs fifty times what a careful one does. Making it harsher was tried and measured: it punishes careful play just as much, so it was left alone. Worth feeling before we change it.
